<Block kind="explain" title="Balance transfers and inspect assumptions">



<Frames>


<Frame title="The ideal water-only balance">

Both portions are the same material; no state changes occur. Lesson 3 pours warm into cool water in the insulated cup. A cup initially near the cool temperature can gain energy and lower the final reading compared with the water-only prediction. Pouring into a warm cup could reverse that contribution; the residual sign is not fixed by conservation alone. Ignore the container, thermometer, heat exchange with surroundings and stirring work. Let warm water give away a **positive magnitude** $Q_w$, while cool water receives positive $Q_c$.

$$m_w c(T_w-T_f)=m_c c(T_f-T_c).$$

The temperature differences here are positive magnitudes. This is equivalent to the sum of signed transfers to the water being zero. Do not mix signed Q with positive magnitudes halfway through a solution.

</Frame>




<Frame title="The midpoint requires equal masses">

For the same water, $c$ cancels. Rearranging gives

$$T_f=\frac{m_wT_w+m_cT_c}{m_w+m_c}.$$

Equal masses give the arithmetic midpoint. Unequal masses give a **mass-weighted** temperature closer to the larger portion’s initial temperature. The result must be between the two initial temperatures under these assumptions. In kelvin, equal masses give (293.15 + 313.15)/2 = 303.15 K, which is the same 30 °C. A weighted mean shifts by the scale offset too.

</Frame>




<Frame title="What a discrepancy tells us">

Using a final reading, calculate $Q_c=m_cc(T_f-T_c)$ and $Q_w=m_wc(T_w-T_f)$. Their difference $R=Q_w-Q_c$ is a **water-only balance residual**. A positive R says the warm-water decrease exceeds the cool-water increase in this calculation.

It is not automatically the exact heat lost to the room. Energy may warm the cup and thermometer; readings, masses and timing may also affect R. Conservation concerns a sufficiently complete system and all transfers, not equality of two incomplete estimates.

</Frame>


</Frames>



</Block>

<Block kind="summary" title="Balance, boundary, evidence">

The midpoint needs equal masses of the same material and an ideal balance. Otherwise use the mass-weighted formula. Calculate both water changes with one stated sign convention. A residual asks you to check the boundary and assumptions; it does not show energy vanished. Keep real evidence separate from synthetic practice.

<Block kind="teacher" title="Discuss the model before a verdict">

**45-minute route:** recall 4 min; prediction and boundary 5; balance frames 10; unequal-mass model 5; worked calculations 8; checks 8; conclusion 5. The reversed-mass extension can be follow-up. Timing is untested.

Use the first stable reading consistently, with actual masses and both initial temperatures. A residual rounded to a joule is arithmetic for this practice set, not a claim of one-joule experimental precision. Discuss instrument resolution before interpreting small differences.
