<Block kind="hook" title="Will the mixture be exactly halfway?">

Your class will mix equal measured masses of cool water near 20 °C and warm water at or below 40 °C. Before touching the equipment, predict the final temperature and list what could make a real result differ. “It should be 30 °C” is a model prediction, not permission to write 30 into the results table.

</Block>




<Block kind="explain" title="Plan a safe and interpretable test">



<Frames>


<Frame title="System and question">

Choose the two portions of water together as the calculation system. Ask: “How close is the measured mixture temperature to an ideal heat-balance prediction?” The simple model omits energy changes of the cup, thermometer and surroundings. Record what is omitted before the experiment; do not decide the assumptions after seeing a result.

</Frame>




<Frame title="Equipment and safety">

**Teacher-supervised classroom activity only.** Use two stable cups, an insulated mixing cup, a balance, a mercury-free thermometer suitable for water (preferably a digital probe), a timer, water and a spill cloth. Aim for about 100 g in each portion. The teacher prepares warm water at **35–40 °C, never above 40 °C**, and checks its temperature before distribution. Pupils do not operate kettles or heat water.

Keep cups away from desk edges and electrical equipment. Do not drink the water. Stir gently without splashing; report spills and dry the surface. If glass breaks, stop, keep away and let the teacher follow the school’s clean-up procedure. If safe equipment or supervision is unavailable, analyse the clearly labelled example data in lesson 4 instead; do not improvise a home heating experiment.

</Frame>




<Frame title="Which quantities to record">

Measure each water mass; do not replace it with the intended “100 g”. Tare the container or subtract its empty mass. Record thermometer and balance resolution (smallest displayed step or scale division), not an invented uncertainty. Put the cool water into the insulated mixing cup; record that cup’s initial condition. Use one thermometer: measure cool water first, then warm water, allowing it to settle each time; record the order and times. Pour warm water into the cool water in that same insulated cup immediately after the warm reading. Measure both initial temperatures as close to mixing as practical.

Use the same protocol in each trial: container, stirring, reading criterion and timing. A changed mass is a changed input, so it must appear in the record and prediction.

</Frame>




<Frame title="Define the reading time">

For this protocol, use this operational rule: while stirring gently, the display stays within one displayed step for 5 s. Record that reading as the **first stable reading** and $t_{\mathrm{reading}}=0$. This criterion does not prove exact thermal equilibrium. The separate pouring clock starts at the start of pouring. Note how many seconds elapsed from pouring to that reading. Then record the temperature at +30 s and +60 s. Keep the thermometer immersed appropriately, without touching the cup wall or bottom, and follow its manufacturer’s instructions.

For a detailed residual comparison, use a thermometer with 0.1 °C resolution or finer and check its documented accuracy; resolution alone is not accuracy. A 1 °C thermometer supports only a rough temperature-range comparison. A later drift can indicate ongoing exchange with the surroundings; it cannot on its own measure the heat exchanged during the whole mixing process.

</Frame>


</Frames>



</Block>

<Block kind="summary" title="A good record survives a surprising result">

Record actual inputs, instrument resolution, first stable reading and its delay. Keep the same protocol in two trials. A prediction and a measurement serve different purposes. A lower-than-predicted reading invites analysis of assumptions; it is not a reason to rewrite the data.

<Block kind="teacher" title="Prepare before the class">

**Physical core:** one supervised trial after protocol preparation. Repeat, unequal masses and extra worked examples are extensions. Assign measuring, recording and timing roles; rotate them if repeating. The apparatus diagram is a schematic; the notebook fields are raw readings. Only lesson 3 needs physical apparatus.

**Before use:** perform the protocol yourself with the actual cups, balance and thermometer; confirm stable-reading response time and handling. No laboratory trial or teacher sign-off is claimed. Water limit ≤40 °C is this protocol’s chosen condition; follow your school’s procedures too.

**Optional expanded two-trial route:** readiness and prediction 7 min; equipment/safety and time definition 8; two supervised trials 20; record check 5; limitations and exit response 5. Allocate analysis to lesson 4. If two safe trials cannot fit, retain a complete single trial and schedule the second later rather than rushing.

The readiness questions assess procedural understanding, not competence with real equipment. Review the submitted record directly; the website cannot automatically judge physical execution or written evidence.
