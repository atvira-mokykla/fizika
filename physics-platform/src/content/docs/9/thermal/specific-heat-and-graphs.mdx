<Block kind="explain" title="Build the model with units">



<Frames>


<Frame title="What specific heat capacity means">

Specific heat capacity $c$ is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of a material by 1 K without a change of state. Different materials have different values. For our liquid-water model, use $c=4200\ \mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}$. This is a useful approximation for these modest temperature ranges, not a universal constant for every state or temperature. For a temperature difference, J/(kg·K) and J/(kg·°C) are equivalent units.

</Frame>




<Frame title="The calculation">

For unchanged state and approximately constant $c$,

$$Q=mc\Delta T,\qquad \Delta T=T_f-T_i.$$

Here $Q$ is **positive when the water gains energy as heat**. Put mass in kg. A difference may be in K or °C because the steps have the same size. The unit check is $\mathrm{kg}\times\mathrm{J/(kg\cdot K)}\times\mathrm{K}=\mathrm{J}$.

</Frame>




<Frame title="Cooling and the model boundary">

For cooling, final − initial is negative, so the signed heat transfer to the water is negative. The **magnitude of heat given away** is its positive opposite. State which quantity you report.

Do not use $mc\Delta T$ to describe melting at constant temperature: energy can change the state even while ΔT is zero. We will treat phase changes later. Real heating can also warm the container and surroundings; energy supplied by a heater need not all reach the water.

</Frame>


</Frames>



</Block>

<Block kind="summary" title="Five checks before substituting">

Identify the system; check that its state stays the same; convert mass to kg; calculate final − initial; give the answer with a unit and sign convention. In the graph, distinguish a reading, a change and a slope. $c$ describes the material; changing mass does not change its specific heat capacity.

<Block kind="teacher" title="Teaching route and misconceptions">

**Investigation core:** prediction 5 min; controlled model comparison 10; discussion and equation 8; one worked example and transfer 7. Leave the rest of the 45-minute slot for transitions and teacher support. These are provisional timings, not classroom measurements. The model comes before the equation explanation; it does not empirically establish the law.

**Expanded practice route, optional:** recall 4 min; prediction 4; equation frames 9; graph model 8; examples 10; checks 8; exit boundary sketch 2. The reverse calculation and written challenge can become follow-up practice. These timings need classroom validation.

Ask pupils to predict before changing the model’s mass, and justify with a ratio before calculating. Compare ΔT and final T explicitly. Typical errors: grams substituted as kg; final temperature used for ΔT; larger c interpreted as larger temperature rise; cooling sign omitted; plateau declared “no energy transfer”.

**Terms EN · LT:** specific heat capacity · savitoji šiluma; temperature change · temperatūros pokytis; slope · nuolydis; heat received/given away · gautas/atiduotas šilumos kiekis. The checker accepts J and kJ but requires the numerical value to match the chosen unit.

No physical heater is needed. The data and visualisation are ideal-model values, not measurements. This addresses only the constant-state and graph foundations within the much broader changes-of-state programme node. Human review is pending.
